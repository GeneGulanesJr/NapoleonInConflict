# The Bridge of Lodi: documentary script (draft 1)

Format: 16:9, about 10 minutes, narrated, with on-screen captions and source cards. Tone: plain and measured; no invented quotations. **Every factual line traces to `lodi-sources.md`.** Lines in `[brackets]` are notes to the director and are not spoken. A tag like `(C)` marks a contested point; the narration must keep the doubt in.

Narration is about 1,600 words (roughly 10 minutes at a calm 160 words a minute, with pauses for the footage).

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00 to 0:45)
[VISUAL: dusk over the Adda, slow push in on the bridge from the Austrian bank; the guns firing along it; nothing yet moves on the span. ON SCREEN: "10 May 1796"]

> On the evening of the tenth of May, 1796, a column of French soldiers stood in the gate of a small Lombard town and looked at a wooden bridge.
>
> It was about two hundred yards long. At the far end stood a line of Austrian guns.
>
> What happened next made a young general famous. It is also very hard to describe. The accounts we have disagree about how many men were there, how many guns, how the attack began, and who went first.
>
> This is what we know, what we don't, and a reconstruction of the parts in between.

[ON SCREEN, small, bottom right throughout: "RECONSTRUCTION: positions and movements are illustrative"]

## 1. The campaign (0:45 to 2:15)
[VISUAL: top-down map of northern Italy, the French route drawn on it; cuts to the western bank of the Po. Use the engine's top-down camera over the Lodi map for the final image.]

> A month earlier the French Army of Italy had been a ragged, half-starved force under a general of twenty-six. In the middle of April Bonaparte beat the Austrians and Piedmontese at Montenotte, Millesimo and Dego, then Mondovì, and on the twenty-eighth Piedmont signed an armistice and left the war.
>
> That left Beaulieu, the Austrian commander, with the River Po to defend. An armistice clause let the French cross it at Valenza, and Beaulieu prepared for that. Bonaparte sent two divisions towards Valenza as a feint, and marched the rest of the army some eighty kilometres east to Piacenza.
>
> On the seventh of May the advance guard under Dallemagne crossed the Po there. Lannes is reported as the first Frenchman on the north bank.
>
> In the next two days there was fighting at Fombio and Codogno. At Codogno, in the dark, the French general Laharpe was killed; one account says he was shot by his own men. Berthier took over, and by the ninth the French were across the Po and the Austrian main army was falling back behind the River Adda.
>
> One town stood on the near side of the Adda, with a bridge. Its name was Lodi.

## 2. The rearguard (2:15 to 3:30)
[VISUAL: the Austrian position, panning along the east bank: batteries first, then the regiments in two lines. ON SCREEN: lower thirds for the units, with strengths from the order of battle.]

> Beaulieu himself was not at Lodi. He had left a rearguard under Feldmarschall-Leutnant Karl Sebottendorf to cover the crossing while the rest of the army got away.
>
> How large was it? One careful order of battle gives 6,577 men. Others give nine and a half thousand, ten thousand, even twelve. The larger figures probably include troops posted elsewhere along the river.
>
> What is clearer is the arrangement. Croat border infantry of the Carlstädter regiments, the Warasdiners and the Nádasdy regiment stood in the first line; the Terzi, Belgiojoso and Thurn regiments in the second, with hussars and uhlans behind. Along the bank were the guns.
>
> How many? The numbers in the sources run from twelve to fourteen. Bonaparte, in his own report, said thirty. Six of them, we are told, stood on a slight rise facing the bridge, and the rest were placed to fire along the approaches and across it.
>
> The bridge itself was wooden, built on piles driven into the river bed, perhaps two hundred yards long and about ten wide. The Austrians tried to destroy it. French artillery firing along its length stopped them.

[ON SCREEN: "Austrian strength: 6,577 to about 12,000, depending on the source"]

## 3. The morning (3:30 to 4:20)
[VISUAL: the French advance guard coming into Lodi from the west, grenadiers clearing the streets, hussars. The gate. First shots across the bridge.]

> The French advance guard reached Lodi before nine in the morning. Lannes's grenadiers and Marmont's hussars cleared the town of the last Austrians and found that the Austrians had crossed the bridge and turned round on the far side.
>
> The advance guard could not force it alone. The first attempt to push on was stopped by canister, and they drew back to wait.
>
> It would be nine hours before the bridge was attacked in earnest.

## 4. The afternoon (4:20 to 5:30)
[VISUAL: the French batteries coming up along the west bank and opening; smoke over the river; a dispatch of cavalry riding off north along the bank.]

> Bonaparte arrived around midday. He brought the army's artillery up to the river, about twenty-four guns by one account, and for several hours they fired across the water at the Austrian line.
>
> He also sent his cavalry under Beaumont upstream to find a way across. How far they had to go is uncertain. One account says they had to travel much further than expected.
>
> Whether he waited for them, or decided not to, is disputed. One historian says he waited for the horse, and then at about six o'clock committed his infantry anyway.

[ON SCREEN: "French guns: about 24 to 30. Austrian guns: 12 to 30."]

## 5. The bridge (5:30 to 7:45)
[VISUAL: the column forming in the gate; Bonaparte addressing the men (commander figure, no words shown). Then the bridge, in two beats: the first rush, then the second. SLOW MOTION on the moment the column stops. CUT to the water, men wading. Caption quotes appear one at a time.]

> At about six o'clock the column formed up in the streets: first the second battalion of carabiniers, and behind them the grenadiers. Bonaparte spoke to the men.
>
> The carabiniers came out of the gate and onto the bridge, and the Austrian guns opened on them with canister.
>
> They got about as far as the middle, and there the column stopped.
>
> We have this from several sources, including Bonaparte's own report, written the next day and printed in London two weeks later. He wrote: *the head of the column seemed to hesitate; through a moment of hesitation perhaps all might have been lost.* That sentence is a translation of the original French, which we have not seen.
>
> Then, he says, Berthier, Masséna, Cervoni, Dallemagne, Lannes and Colonel Dupas *precipitated themselves at the head of a column, and decided the day.*
>
> Here the accounts split. Some say the first wave fell back and a second attacked. Others describe one attack that faltered and then went on. Some say the carabiniers were first; others the grenadiers. We do not know how far the first group got, or for how long they stood.
>
> What the accounts do share is the water. Some of the men climbed down among the piles and waded through the shallows beside the bridge, firing up at the gunners. Vigo-Roussillon, a grenadier who was there, remembered a single Austrian salvo as the column was part way across.
>
> However it happened, they reached the far end. The Austrian guns were taken.

[ON SCREEN: "Accounts differ: one attack or two? Carabiniers first, or grenadiers?"]

## 6. The far bank (7:45 to 8:45)
[VISUAL: the east bank in confusion; the Austrian infantry advancing; Masséna's and Augereau's men coming over the bridge; cavalry at the edge of the frame crossing the river upstream.]

> On the far bank Sebottendorf counter-attacked and very nearly pushed the French back into the river. Masséna's troops, and then Augereau's, arrived in time to hold.
>
> Then Beaumont's cavalry appeared. They had found a ford, though by Bonaparte's own account it was fairly deep, the crossing was slow, and the horse could not charge.
>
> The Austrians withdrew towards Crema and Cremona in the dusk. Bonaparte's report says that darkness, and the exhaustion of his men, stopped the pursuit.

## 7. What it was worth (8:45 to 10:00)
[VISUAL: slow dawn over the bridge, men and horses dead on the planks, the river. Cut to a map of Lombardy; the road to Milan. Closing wide shot.]

> The losses are as hard to pin down as everything else. Bonaparte said his own were *inconsiderable.* Most modern accounts give about 350. One gives a thousand, another fifteen hundred. The Austrians lost between two and three thousand men, killed, wounded and taken, and between fifteen and twenty guns.
>
> Beaulieu's army escaped. Within a week Masséna was in Milan, and Bonaparte entered it on the fifteenth.
>
> Was Lodi necessary? One historian says Beaulieu was already retreating and the bridge could probably have been taken by waiting. Another calls it little more than a skirmish.
>
> But Bonaparte's soldiers began to call him *the little corporal*, a name tied in most accounts to this campaign, though nobody can say exactly when. The day after the battle he wrote to Carnot that *the battle of Lodi gives the Republic all of Italy.* And years later he said Lodi had given him *the first spark of high ambition*; a recollection from afterwards, not a record from the time.
>
> Perhaps that is what mattered. Historians who doubt the battle's military value still say it convinced Bonaparte of what he could do, and showed his men that he could ask them for a costly attack, and get it.

## 8. End card (10:00 to 10:30)
[ON SCREEN: a plain card.]

> *This film is a reconstruction. The positions and movements of individual units are illustrative; the order of battle follows the published accounts, and the places where those accounts disagree are marked. Sources are listed in the description.*

## Production notes (not part of the script)
- **Voice:** the Windows speech engine used for the Shorts is too rough for ten minutes of documentary. Either a recorded voice, or a better synthesised one; the captions and timings below are independent of which.
- **The reconstruction:** the sim is directed by scripted French orders at the times above (guns open, carabiniers form and rush, hesitate, second rush, ford, counter-attack, withdrawal), so the footage follows the narration. It is **not** a bot-vs-bot result.
- **Beaulieu's absence, Laharpe's death, the Valenza feint and the Kellermann dispute** are narrated from the written sources only; no footage is claimed for them.
- **Things deliberately left out:** the "superior man" quote (unverified), the claim that Bonaparte laid the guns himself (no contemporary evidence), and the claim that the Austrians had broken the bridge (they tried and were stopped).
- **Before release:** read Vigo-Roussillon's *Journal de campagne*, Boycott-Brown and Chandler, and check the Directory letter against the French original.
